Semantic HTML — Give the Page Meaning

The broken page is built entirely out of <div> elements. Replace them with meaningful HTML5 landmarks so screen readers can navigate the page.

What you are fixing

A screen reader user lands on this page and presses the H key to jump to a heading, or R to jump to a landmark region. Right now, nothing happens — there are no landmarks. Every structural element is a <div>, which carries zero meaning. Blind users must listen to the entire page from top to bottom, every time.

Why this matters

Landmarks are the screen reader's table of contents

When a screen reader user presses a shortcut key (e.g., R in NVDA, W in VoiceOver), they jump to the next landmark region on the page. Without landmarks, they hear the entire page read aloud from start to finish — every time they visit.

  • <header> — identifies the site header (mapped to the "banner" ARIA role)
  • <nav> — marks a navigation region; add aria-label when you have more than one
  • <main> — the primary content; there should be exactly one per page
  • <section> — a thematic grouping; becomes a landmark when it has an aria-label or aria-labelledby
  • <article> — a self-contained piece of content (a movie review, a blog post) that makes sense on its own
  • <aside> — content that is related but secondary (sidebars, editor's picks)
  • <footer> — the site footer (mapped to the "contentinfo" ARIA role)

Also change <div class="section-title">Now Showing</div> to <h2>Now Showing</h2> — headings are another navigation shortcut, and the broken page has only one heading (<h1>) for the whole page.

Quick tip — ARIA roles vs. HTML elements: You can achieve the same effect with role="main" on a <div>, but native HTML elements are preferred. They carry the role implicitly, require less typing, and are more robust across different assistive technologies.
